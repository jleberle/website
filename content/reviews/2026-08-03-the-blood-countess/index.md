As someone who regularly bought copies of *The Guinness Book of World Records* at the Scholastic Book Fair growing up, I honestly can't remember seeing Elizabeth Bathory or the idea of the Blood Countess show up. Yet as Shelley Puhak documents, Bathory's supposed legacy as one of the worst serial killers has long outlived the reality of her time.
<!-- more -->
According to the legends, Elizabeth Bathory was a powerful Hungarian noble, who with the help of her servants allegedly killed up to 650 young girls as part of a prolonged campaign of torture to seemingly keep herself young and vibrant. Bathory's alleged crimes, however, were entirely fictional, the work of a frustrated priest and the political fighting that dominated a region caught between the Habsburgs and the Ottomans on either side. Bathory's family was one of the largest landholders in greater Hungary with properties stretching from the Bratislava area through the mountains of Transylvania. Elizabeth, as a widow, controlled a large swath of valuable lands and properties, which made her an enticing target.

The end result were sensational stories of Elizabeth bathing in the blood of virgin girls, torturing girls with the help of her servants, and conducting mass secret burials of the bodies. Much of the claims came from people who worked for the Countess who proclaimed increasingly outlandish stories under the duress of repeated violent torture. Even though the evidence did not support the facts of the case, Elizabeth and her servants were ultimately convicted for the alleged crimes with the servants being executed and Elizabeth imprisoned until she died.

Elizabeth's downfall largely arose from two different issues. First the land divide and the need to weaken a powerful family within the Hungarian region seemed to provide little reason for allowing Elizabeth to mount a serious defense even though she more than had the resources to. Secondly, Hungary at the time was increasingly divided religiously between the Lutherans and the Calvinists. Whereas Elizabeth was raised a Calvinist, her determined prosecutor, György Thurzó, was a Lutheran. As was the disgruntled priest near her castle, who found himself regularly passed over for promotions and saw attacking Elizabeth as a way to win support. 

Puhak's book isn't the first to deal with the legends of the Blood Countess and she acknowledges a debt to previous scholars who have begun to walk back the folklore surrounding Bathory. Even before Puhak's book came out the *Guinness Book of World Records* revised their passage on Bathory to note that the claims are so wrapped up in fiction that it's impossible to prove. Puhak and others have shown pretty clearly that the stories were the work of an overzealous investigator and based on little in reality. Puhak's most interesting part is probably her discussion of the nature of burials between Lutherans and Calvinists, the latter of which engaged in private burials which many labeled as "secret." Puhak shows that the mistranslations over the years have misinterpreted those secret but normal burials as some kind of plot to hide the bodies of Bathory's victims. 

Puhak's book is weighed down at times by the sheer amount of people and various political movements swirling around Bathory's life between two empires. Additionally I found it frustrating that the discussion of how Bathory became the Blood Countess of folklore was confined to the epilogue, leaving me for much of the book questioning about the sensational stories, which didn't attract all that much attention during her life, become something that appeared in *Guinness* centuries later.
