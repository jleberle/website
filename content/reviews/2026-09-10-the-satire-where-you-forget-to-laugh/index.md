Normally a book like *Yesteryear* would probably be something I'd skip over, never giving it much thought when I passed it in the bookstore or saw someone reading it around. Yet this book has been pretty much everywhere, and has received [positive endorsement](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/05/10/briefing/yesteryear-caro-claire-burke.html) from various publications. Plus the book deals with the ideas of historical memory of the American West and touches on the modern online movements that revolve around right-wing politics. So at the same time it feels right up my secluded southwestern Idaho valley. Unfortunately, the book did not live up to the hype and left me far more annoyed than anything else. Be warned, there's spoilers below.
<!-- more -->
The basic premise of *Yesteryear* is that Natalie Heller Mills is a fundamentalist Christian from out west who heads off to college at Harvard only to marry a failson from a political dynasty. Desperate to chart some kind of course for the family, Natalie convinces her father-in-law Doug to finance the family moving to a farm in Idaho. On the farm Natalie slowly builds up a following on Instagram until an Alex Jones-like livestreamer plugs her account as the embodiment of traditional American values. The tradwife persona hides the fact that the account is a business more than a family. The family hires two nannies, a producer, and countless farm hands who all help run the supposedly wholesome, organic family farm. Except the kids don't know what an ocean is, the dishwasher and modern appliances are hidden away, and the farmhands spray pesticides at night because her husband Caleb can't actually grow organic produce.This all seemingly sets up what could be a biting satire of tradwifes, social media influencers, and the modern world we live in. Except the book is so thin on substance it turns into a satire without a satire. Far too much focus turns to the big reveal -- Natalie isn't actually transported back in time, she's in the present trying to live in the past after being canceled online.

The frustrating thing is at no point did I feel any real connections to any of the characters. Should I hate Natalie for exploiting her kids, building a fake life and ruining her family? I guess, but there's not enough to actually make you feel that way. Should I feel anything for the kids in the "past", who really just operate as empty shells? Hard to again. What about the mother-in-law, driven to addiction by her husband's constant campaigning? Hard when she appears and disappears just as quick. None of the plot points really develop to a degree worth following.

Then we get to the inconsistencies that left me feeling like Max Read's [description of Threads after it's release](https://maxread.substack.com/p/threads-is-the-gas-leak-social-network): there's something not right but I can't exactly put my finger on it. Except, after thinking, I can. Burke has created a world that's based on vibes rather than any real basis in reality and that could be an interesting connection to Natalie's unreliable narration but it's never developed. Instead I regularly felt like I was reading a high schooler's English assignment where they try to create a world and it just doesn't make sense.

For instance, let's consider Doug, the father-in-law politician. Doug comes from a long line of Senators and he lives in Napa Valley. Doug's a nine term Senator who wins re-election in the book, weirdly at a campaign rally in Salt Lake City, not California. We never find out Doug's political party but given that he wants Natalie to promote him on her page, talks about traditional family values and a civil war coming, we can generally infer he's a conservative. A nine term conservative Senator in California? Pete Wilson was the last elected Republican Senator from California back in the 1980s. That's not the end of it either, Doug wants Caleb to run for political office and Natalie says it's time after he cheats on her and threatens to run away to Brooklyn with the producer. Doug says they'll have Caleb run in an state senate race in California where the widow of the former senator is running unopposed. So we're going to insert Caleb into an active election when he lives out of state, makes sense. Did I mention the author runs a "culture and politics" podcast?

Or what about the big twist, that Natalie stripped everything out of her house after getting canceled and sought to revert back to a fictional 1855 in a desperate attempt to prove her authenticity? Her and her husband lived like that for 18 years, having three kids, all the while it's implied she's medicated and doesn't remember most of it. The medication comes from one pill bottle her mother-in-law gave her ten years before she was canceled and then black market pills from her sons who are secretly supplying the fictional homestead. 18 years of a clear mental health crisis, being medicated by others, and having kids, and no one called protective services? Her kids from her social media days literally walk off the homestead after she's canceled and nothing happens because her father-in-law is buying people off? The father-in-law who saw his son digging an outhouse hole and immediately left?

What about the fact that when it's all said and done Natalie is the only one we directly see punished for child neglect? Nothing about her husband both in terms of the children or the years of medicating and refusal to report a clearly problematic situation. Or Clementine, the daughter who says she's waited years to get the kids out, ignoring that by the time the story ends she's in her late 20s and certainly had plenty of time to do something. 

Even the location is problematic and inconsistent. The ranch as best we know is somewhere in southwestern Idaho, yet when they fly out to tour the ranch they fly into Sun Valley and drive three hours. It sits in a lush valley with old growth forests. First, if the ranch is in southwestern Idaho, you'd want a really good reason to fly into Sun Valley instead of Boise and southwestern Idaho doesn't exactly have tons of large old growth forests in its arid plateaus. 

Now I can hear the argument, these are small issues, just go with it, it's fiction. Except the issues repeatedly took me out of the story and even fiction should have some basis in reality. Five minutes of work here and there could've made a story that would leave the reader focusing on the actual story and not the holes. Have Doug be a Mitt Romney stand-in. Have the family live in central Idaho because Caleb grow up skiing in Sun Valley.

Normally I don't care to be so negative in a review, but given all the hype I was more than a little surprised at how disappointing the end result of this book is. It's supposed to be satire I guess, but it's lacking the required biting humor, or really any humor, and it largely misses the entire point of satire by failing to actually critique anything in much depth.
