In 2018 I opened Richard Grant's ["When the Socialist Revolution Came to Oklahoma -- and Was Crushed"](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/socialist-revolution-oklahoma-crushed-green-corn-rebellion-180973073/) in the online *Smithsonian Magazine* and was frankly shocked by what I saw. Not the fact that their was socialism in Oklahoma or by the Green Corn Rebellion. No I knew both of those things. What was surprising was the fact that the article opened quoting Ted Eberle, given my grandfather was born in Seminole county, so there seemed a high likelihood he could be related. Then I kept reading and stopped, sitting bolt upright when I read Ted mention Albert Eberle. Eberle isn't that common a last name but there's enough that I don't immediately assume I'm related to an Eberle, but Albert? I knew Albert was my great-grandfather's name. And I knew my grandfather regularly implied the family wasn't a fan of the government coming around but said little else.[^1] Could this really be my great-grandfather?

[^1]: When the 1940 census came out, I asked him if I could find them on the rolls, he said probably not, they'd either have been moving around or would not have wanted to talk to a federal official. 
<!-- more -->

I quickly signed up for ancestry.com and starting building a family tree as a basic first step and what I quickly found out was that the Albert in the article was very much my great-grandfather but not only were him and his brother involved in the rebellion but so was his future father-in-law. Jasper "Mack" Danley, or J.M. also turned up in news reports on ancestry.com and like Albert he also had a Leavenworth inmate photo on his hints page. I checked with relatives that there was a Ted Eberle in the family tree and mostly left it at that. I should've done more digging but being overwhelmed with any number of things, I left it as a fun anecdote to add in lectures. 

Before more on my family, a quick summary of the Green Corn Rebellion. Oklahoma in the 1910s had a large population of tenant farmers, guys and families barely getting by on a yearly basis, something that opened them up to the idea of populism and later socialism as a potential political avenue to push back on the large landowners and companies. By 1916 Oklahoma had a large socialist presence with the Industrial Workers of World regularly working to unionize oil field workers and farm laborers, but those tenant farmers sat in an awkward position. Many socialists argued they were very much not part of the working class and therefore not eligible for membership in the IWW. In stepped the Working Class Union, formed in Arkansas as a way to organize tenant farmers. The WCU built on southern resistance traditions with night-time barn burnings and destruction of cattle dips.[^2] With the outbreak of World War I, Woodrow Wilson implemented the draft and tenant farmers were encouraged by the WCU to resist the draft and the "rich man's war" they were being sent to fight. At the start of August 1917 members decided they'd stage a march across the country to tell Wilson what they thought of the draft. On the way they'd eat green corn and brisket, picking up what they hoped would be scores of like-minded people. 

The rebellion, however, never got out of Seminole County. After an ambush related in a deputy being shot, the rebels moved up their plans for a full scale rebellion, destroying railroad bridges around the Sasakwa area throughout the night. By the next morning with the arrival of additional posse members, the rebellion's motto according to Grant shifted from fight like hell to run like hell as members scattered and were ultimately arrested. Which now brings us back to Albert, his brother Anthony, and Jasper Danley.

[^2]: Texas fever in cattle herds were an increasing problem in the state, so the state mandated dipping cattle to ward off ticks that carried the disease. The dips, however, proved deadly to cattle and small farmers argued their cattle herds were not a major issue for Texas Fever in comparison to the large commercial herds in the western part of the state. For more, see Clark.<!-- cite: @clark1951 -->

I've been digging through a lot of the federal archival material on the rebellion, most of which is digitized through [Fold3](https://fold3.com), [FamilySearch](https://www.familysearch.org/en/united-states/), and the National Archives itself. I've managed to track down a considerable amount of information on the family, their involvement, and the involvement of the larger community which they lived in during the period. What I've found is that the Eberles and Danley were deeply connected to the processes that shaped Indian Territory and Oklahoma during the period and were by all accounts fundamentally involved in the rebellion in 1917. There's still a lot of searching and reading to do, but I doubt Albert, Anthony or J.M. thought they'd be a part of scholarly work.

Most of the material I'm going to keep close at the moment, but I can say the most shocking discovery was the central nature of J.M. to the whole rebellion. J.M. received a ten year prison sentence (although he was released early) which was the max sentence for any defendants in the trials. Grant's article mentioned a Jim Danley, but no Jim Danley appears in any news results of the period. There is a Jim or James Danley mentioned in federal interviews, but that seems to be the result of J.M. being mistranscribed or written as Jim on a regular basis. According to a number of interviews during the period J.M. was one of the leading organizers and the head of the Friendship Local of the WCU that Albert and Anthony joined and according to Grant he was the one who gave the run like hell orders. J.M., meanwhile, told interrogators that there was no leader, members picked a new leader for each meeting and he happened to be one.

The goal is to tell all of their stories and the story of the larger Friendship community by this time next year. If you're related in some way, and want to discuss more about this, please get in touch.
