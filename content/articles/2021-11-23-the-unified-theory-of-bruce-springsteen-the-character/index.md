Driving home the other day one of the DJs on a random SiriusXM station mentioned that they felt Bruce Springsteen was singing about a character in all of his songs. Well, of course. This isn't actually a new idea I said aloud to no one. Springsteen himself acknowledged in his Broadway show that he's made a career out of singing about stuff he's never actually experienced in his life (to his credit the DJ mentioned this). And that is the biggest criticism of Springsteen's music, it's a hollow career of a rich guy playing a poor man. When I taught a class dealing with music and American History the students generally hated Springsteen and many of them talked about how it felt weird for Springsteen to be singing about those topics. 

While Springsteen had made a career of singing about working-class Americans, what he's really done is construct a decades long story of a nameless American living through the legacies of the 1960s, the depressions of the 1970s, and the hollowness of the 1980s success. Springsteen's character could have any job and the basic story would still be the same. The characters of Springsteen's songs, beginning with 1973's *Greetings From Ashbury Park, N.J.* parallels the life of Bruce Springsteen, the real person, but the character is living in the world that begins with Nixon's downfall and culminates with 9/11 and the forever wars of the 21th century. The narrative does get lost after you move past 1987's *Tunnel of Love* but Springsteen (the artist) managed to construct a searing portrait of a fictional career from youthful experimentation throughout middle age depression. None of this is particularly novel or new, but I need to write about something, so might as well write about Springsteen.

Springsteen's two earliest albums, *Greetings* and *The Wild, the Innocent, and the E Street Shuffle* are stories of a kid living in your standard middle-class American town with a young youthful vibrancy to them. The biggest issue is the fact that the character's girlfriend, Rosalita has a dad who doesn't like her boyfriend. But that boyfriend has a new record contract and seems ready to hit the big time. In reality the stories and songs in the first two albums are Springsteen trying to imitate Bob Dylan's winding song structures, but the albums offer a portrait of teenage wonder and imagination as he grows up. 

With *Born to Run* you begin to see the character consider the idea of escaping, leaving the death trap of small town life for something besides driving around at night to burn off the frustrations of the day. And for a period, it seems like he might actually leave. Yet within a few years we found out he's resigned himself to essentially staying in place. *Darkness on the Edge of Town* and *The River* let us know the character is still racing around town and he's married early and taken a job at the factory. With the economy of the late 1970s, there really wasn't an escape, every town seemingly had the same darkness on the edge of town. The character even goes so far as to think about violence, real or imagined as embodied by Springsteen's darkest album, *Nebraska*. Those dark thoughts continue through the 80s as the character confronts the realities of the post-Vietnam era, watching veterans deal with mental health and economic issues, while towns across the US find themselves regularly hollowed as as those factories that provided jobs disappear as companies consolidate and merge throughout the decade.

By the end of the 1980s the issues have simply become too much and the character divorces, all the while longing for a love he simply can't have. After *Tunnel of Love* the narrative does grow more disconnected but *Lucky Town* and *Hungry Heart* do provide a picture of our divorced character grappling with the realities of having time to flip through 57 channels but finding nothing on, leading him to explore deeper subjects like immigration which he would've seen on TV during the 1990s in the aftermath of NAFTA and the ongoing hollowing out of America's blue collar industries. 

This is only a brief idea and Springsteen's real life does appear in the songs as he grappled with issues with his father, his own divorce, and the breakup of the E Street Band in the early 1990s. But taken as a whole Springsteen constructed a narrative arch that followed your blue collar everyman through the trials and tribulations of life in the 1970s and 1980s with the declining economy and then the hollow resurgence under Reagan.
