> [Anthropic] is not the largest company in the AI race. It does not have the distribution of Google or Microsoft. It does not have the brand recognition of OpenAI. So it constructs a moral framework in which being the safety-conscious actor is the superior position, and in which the giants are implicitly transgressive. The mythos of responsibility is also a competitive weapon.

That's a quote from [Om Malik's last blog post](https://om.co/2026/06/07/the-myth-the-mythos-and-the-man/) before he unfortunately passed away this summer dealing with the naming of Anthropic's Mythos model that was released and then restricted and then re-released this summer. Om correctly contented the naming of the model Mythos was part of a slick marketing campaign to position Anthropic as the enlightened choice of AI models. And to be fully honest, it's a good part of the reason I picked Claude as the primary AI model to test this summer. Now that it's August and the summer is ending, it's time to sunset Claude, and I leave it behind more concerned than ever.
<!-- more -->
My two biggest takeaways are very simply that the agents provide an addictiveness to their work and the product can look good but can be deeply problematic in ways I simply can't understand. In terms of the former, as Hank Green said in [his recent video acknowledging AI usage](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ssE1rmt3EpE), every problem became an AI problem. Once I finished the basic rebuilding of my repos I was looking for other things to improve, and then other things after that. The more it did, the more I wanted it to do. The problem, of course, is I don't fully understand what it did.

Cal Newport in a recent blog post quoted a programmer who'd thrown in with AI coding as a way to reduce his project build times [only to have the system backfire](https://calnewport.com/on-ai-coding-and-its-discontents/): 

>He told me that features he generated using Claude Code ended up crashing their product on two different occasions. His boss told him that if it happened one more time, he’d be fired. “I’ve never had quality issues like this before in my career.”
>
>The problem is that code produced by an AI agent _looks_ reasonable, but can contain ‘hard-to-spot bugs’ that end up causing major problems. As a result, you should carefully review your agent’s output, but this is difficult. As the engineer told me, it’s “famously hard” to understand code you didn’t write yourself, so this extra step becomes “easy to just blow it off (especially when we are all trying to ‘10x’ our velocity).” Soon, systems start to break.

The benefit for me is that nothing I use is going to cause me issues if it stops working. If one of my dotfiles configs fails, it fails. If my micro.blog theme runs into errors, my site will have problems. But those are me problems, I'm not actively selling or shipping code with Claude and believe that would be both ethnically problematic and dangerous. If I build a public app, I can't service it, instead I'd be wholly reliant on Claude. That'd be like building cars in my garage based on Youtube videos and then selling them. No one should trust those cars, nor should they trust the code Claude wrote this summer. While the failures will be confined to me, I am sitting in a rather rickety car that may or may not fail with no ability to fix it. Plus I have no incentive to learn to fix it with my loyal AI companion always around.

Which brings me to the issue that bit the programmer and Hank Green, AI is seductive and it encourages you to continue using it. The more you use it, the more you want to use. But the more you use it, you lose actual skills. Hank Green admitted he used AI as a help for planning and brainstorming, which slowly spiraled into more usage. I never had Claude touch anything I wrote but did I regularly ask it to do file movement or other simple tasks I could've done myself by hand? Of course, because everything became a Claude problem. Over time more and more become Claude problems because it's easier to simply feed the machine a prompt than do something yourself. But doing things yourself builds a muscle memory, without that muscle memory things start to fall apart and become harder to do on your own. Those Claude problems can ultimately became major problems if you're not careful.

The programmer Cal Newport cited almost got fired, while Terry Godier found himself at the center of an [internet firestorm because of Claude](https://daringfireball.net/2026/08/retraction_app_store_rejection_of_the_week). Godier, developer of the RSS app Current, wrote [a blog post](https://blog.terrygodier.com/2026/08/07/browsers-have-standards-the-app.html) talking about how one of his apps was rejected from the app store for reasons that made no sense. Except…24 hours later, they made perfect sense. Godier wrote an astrology app and submitted it to the App Store, which got rejected. So he wrote an astronomy app with Claude [that copied code verbatim from another app](https://bsky.app/profile/terrygodier.com/post/3ms2lm4kcfc2j). He's since acknowledged ["irresponsible use of AI to build such a thing"](https://blog.terrygodier.com/2026/08/09/mea-culpa-dark-hours.html). All of the agents are large language models, they're not intelligent, they're simply building things based on what has come before. So anything in my repos could very well draw from another source and Claude won't bother to credit it because it's only goal is to make me happy, saying no doesn't usually do that.[^1]

This gets me to Bruce Schneier article about the [gym vs. work](https://www.theguardian.com/commentisfree/2026/jul/24/should-you-use-ai):

> But it’s precisely because I have spent years developing my own writing skills that I’m able to identify prose that sounds great but doesn’t actually make sense. My students don’t have that skill; they mistakenly view a confident, well-written essay as evidence of the quality of their ideas. They see the AI as cleaning those ideas up, getting them through that uncomfortable stretch of having to turn those ideas into prose. What the students miss is that their initial discomfort is a normal and healthy stage of writing, and not something to quickly get beyond. The very act of struggling with how to express what they think is an important part of the process. It’s how they test out their ideas, examine their hypotheses, and actually figure out what they think. Homework is not work; it’s the gym.

Writing code for me is work, these updates were moving heavy things around and using a forklift to do it. Anything I write professionally? That's the gym, something you need to train and keep the muscles present for. That's why I was clear with Claude that all public facing content was explicitly mine to edit and it was restricted from the content folder except for cleaning up frontmatter. I didn't even have it proofread posts or offer suggestions on them. If they suck, that's me, not the robots talking.

[^1]: This actually happened once with my [bookmarklets](https://github.com/jleberle/bookmarklets). I wanted a bookmarklet to post to Micro.blog with a standard format and Claude built it, copying code from another repository which it labeled "official." When I caught it I pushed back and it's answer was to rewrite the code without attribution, so plagiarism. As mentioned in my repos, if something Claude has done is in need of attribution, please do get [in touch](/about#connect) so I can add it. 

Then, of course, there's the real world impacts. The water and electrical usage of data centers. The centers that provide a regular constant hum. The increased cost of RAM and processors to build out new data centers, many of which are built in lower income communities or rural areas ill-equipped to handle the new demands but desperate for jobs. This summer has seen record heat in Europe, rivers drying up, and fires that ravaged Spain, France, Spokane, and Canada. Summers are growing hotter, longer, and drier, while winters grow shorter, warmer, and drier. My individual AI usage was a minuscule part of the larger demand from corporations but it's still something. [There may be ways to reduce the environmental costs](https://newsletter.dancohen.org/archive/how-can-we-minimize-the-environmental-impact-of-our-ai-use/) but those will require more testing before they can reasonably be defined as actual reductions.

So, yes, as summer comes to an end, I've done a number of things I've wanted to for a while and find myself excited to dive into some old material during the semester. Yet, I'm deeply troubled by the seductiveness of the systems and the reliance that many are building into their daily lives.
